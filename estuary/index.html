This is my rewrite of the Project Estuary deck, written the way I'd write it as a founding partner. The deck is careful and honest, and starting small on the Gulf Coast with two rods is the right call. The weak spot is the product. Two spinning rods with Fuji guides and a cork grip describe almost every inshore rod sold between $150 and $300, so a customer, a shop or a future buyer has no particular reason to pick this one.

I've added three features that give it a reason, and redone the cost model with the duty rates that apply in late 2026. The numbers come out tighter than the deck shows.

Status: concept only. There are no prototypes, supplier quotes, sales or partners yet. Anything marked as an estimate is my own figure and still needs checking.

The three additions

1. Every rod is measured at the factory and ships with its own numbers on a card.

2. Hardware and service built around how rods fail in salt water.

3. Later, a handle that unlocks and moves between blanks.

The product

Rods in this price range share the same blank materials, Fuji guides and cork. On a rack they look interchangeable. The brand needs things a customer can see and check, and things the company ends up owning.

A measured card in every tube launch

Each rod goes on a simple rig at the factory. We record its weight to 0.1 g, its balance point with a standard 2500 reel, how far the tip drops under a 1 lb weight, and the action angle that results. Those numbers go on a numbered card in the tube and on a web page for that production batch.

Power and action ratings aren't standardized, so one brand's medium can be another's medium-light. A measurement gets around that. The company also builds up a record of every rod it has made, which helps quality control and is something a buyer can examine.

A big brand could copy the card, but most don't publish how much their rods vary. My guess is the numbers wouldn't flatter them.

Roughly $0.60–1.50 a rod, plus $400–900 for the rig.

Built for salt water launch

Inshore rods mostly fail in a few ways: a broken tip, a cracked guide insert, a corroded reel seat, a car door. The design goes after those.

  • Silicon carbide rings on titanium frames as the upgrade
  • No bare aluminum touching stainless steel, which corrodes in salt
  • An O-ring in the reel seat hood, and a fore grip sealed to the blank
  • Spare tip tops and guides for sale, with re-wraps done by partner shops
  • A set price for a replacement blank after a break

Warranty claims cost less to handle this way, and the claim records show what to fix next.

Keel Lock handle phase 2, after testing

A machined collar locks the handle and reel seat onto a ground section at the base of the blank. A key lines the reel up with the guides. One handle then fits both the Flats and the Channel blank.

A second rod costs the angler $129 for a blank instead of $219. The rod packs shorter, and existing customers have something new to buy.

Detachable handles have been around for decades, so a utility patent may not be possible. It ships only if it passes the fatigue and break tests in section 3. If it doesn't, the line stays fixed-handle.

Brand

Tide and river gauges are marked with a repeating E shape every tenth of a foot. Anyone who fishes tidal water has seen one bolted to a piling. The logo uses that E, so the brand's mark is a measuring mark.

A. Gauge E (my pick). Readable at 6 mm on a butt cap. Repeated in a row, it becomes the pattern on the packaging.

B. Confluence. A river and the tide join into a line that ends in a tip-top ring. Friendlier, but closer to the wave logos everyone already uses.

C. Batch seal. A second mark for the spec card, the batch page and the rod tube end cap.

Colors

Marsh#13201F

Tannin is the tea color that marsh water turns. It's the standard blank color, and no big brand uses it in this category. Gauge red comes from the numbers painted on tide gauges.

Check the name first

"Estuary" describes where the rod gets used. Names like that can be refused registration or be hard to enforce. Search before printing anything.

  • Keep ESTUARY if the search and an attorney's opinion come back clean
  • If not, use a made-up company name and keep Estuary as the inshore line
  • Made-up names to search, none checked yet: Tidewright, Brackline, Saltgauge

Search at USPTO trademark search, Class 28 (fishing tackle).

How it talks

Plain and specific, with numbers in place of adjectives.

  • "Insane sensitivity" becomes "4.1 oz on our scale. Here's the scale."
  • "Built tough" becomes "Batch 003's average breaking load, and how we tested it."
  • "Limited drop!" becomes "Batch 004 is 300 rods in Tannin. The next batch is Graphite."

What it won't do

  • Invent scarcity, or post gifted reviews without saying so, or buy reviews
  • Say "first" or "best" without a source
  • Put pro-staff patches on people who don't fish the rods

Factories and cost

I haven't contacted any factory. The duty notes below reflect what was reported in November 2026 and need a licensed customs broker to confirm. The new Section 301 duties are being challenged in court and could change again.

Taiwan

Long experience with rods and blanks, and flexible with prototypes. Labor costs more. Reported duty: the regular rate plus Section 301, capped near 10% combined.

Ask them for engineering help. Model setting: 10%.

Vietnam

A growing rod export base. Check that the blanks are actually rolled there, because a Chinese blank finished in Vietnam can raise origin questions. Reported duty: 6% regular plus 10–12.5% Section 301.

Main quote. Model setting: 18.5%.

China

The most suppliers (the rod factories around Weihai) and usually the lowest factory price. It also has the most duty stacked up: regular, the older Section 301 duties and the new ones.

Get a quote to compare even if you buy elsewhere. Model setting: 35% (range about 24–44%).

From quote request to first shipment

  1. Sign the NNN agreement, then send drawings (Figs. 1–3), parts list and test plan
  2. Pay for samples from the top two factories. Plan on 2–3 rounds.
  3. Measure the samples on our rig next to six competitor rods
  4. Sign off on one golden sample and freeze the spec
  5. Order a pilot run of 300–500, with an inspection company checking it before it ships (ISO 2859-1, AQL 2.5 major, 4.0 minor)
  6. Ship by sea, clear customs through a broker, and pay excise tax quarterly on IRS Form 720

Factories close for weeks around Lunar New Year in February 2027. Samples requested in January may not ship until March.

Marketing

Inshore anglers mostly trust the guides they've fished with and the shops where they buy bait, so marketing starts there. Paid ads come later, once there's something worth pointing them at. The partners below are roles to fill. Nobody has been contacted.

6–10 guides

Guide fleet

Working inshore guides on the middle Texas coast, the Louisiana marsh, Florida's Gulf coast and around Charleston or Georgia. Each gets a set of rods and a paid testing contract. Clients who buy with the guide's code earn the guide 10%.

You get real wear-and-tear data, and each guide puts the rods in 100+ clients' hands a season.

2–4 creators

Fishing channels

YouTube or Instagram creators with 10,000–150,000 followers who actually fish the Gulf. A flat fee for three videos plus 8% of sales, disclosed on every post as the FTC requires. Estuary can reuse the footage indefinitely.

Read their comments to see whether the audience fishes.

1 advisor

One long-term partner

After six months of working together, one respected guide or rod builder might get 0.5–2% of the company. That share vests over four years, with nothing in the first year, and comes with real design input and their name on one batch.

Don't give equity for one-off posts.

10 shops

Independent dealers

Two demo rods and a printed batch sheet per shop, plus a casting day each season. Start on consignment or small orders and check sales every month.

Reorders are the number that shows real demand.

What to post about

  • The measuring rig: competitor rods, batch numbers, failures
  • Tide notes: what works by region and season
  • Repairs: rinsing, re-wrapping, replacing a tip
  • Build log: factory visits and sample rounds, rejects included

Making people want one

  • Numbered batches in real quantities, each in one color
  • Tannin as a color people recognize from across a dock
  • A page for every batch, so owners can look up their rod
  • Measuring days at shops where anglers bring any rod to be measured

Numbers to watch

  • Cost of a first order, by channel
  • Share of sales through guide codes
  • How fast shops sell through, and how often they reorder
  • Warranty claims per 100 rods
  • Customers buying again within 12 months

What makes it sellable

  • Trademark registered in the US in Class 28, and filed in the main export markets
  • Design patents on the handle and collar, and a utility patent only if a search shows the idea is new
  • Contracts saying the company owns the mandrels, layups and tooling
  • Two approved factories in different countries
  • The batch measurements and warranty history
  • No single retailer above 30% of sales
  • Accrual bookkeeping from the first day, with reviewed statements by Year 3
  • Rights to reuse all creator footage, and the company's own email list

Who might buy

Big tackle groups such as Pure Fishing (sold by Newell Brands in 2018 for about $1.3B), Rapala VMC, Johnson Outdoors and Globeride, which owns Daiwa. Also distributors who want their own brand, private equity firms building outdoor portfolios, and retailers who would rather buy a brand than build a store brand.

What they look for is a brand that's growing in shops they don't already reach, with margins that still work after their own overhead.
